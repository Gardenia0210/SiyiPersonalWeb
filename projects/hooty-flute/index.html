01 — RESEARCH

Understanding the therapy gap

Pediatric dysphagia therapy demands repetition, professional precision, and emotional safety. Interviews and contextual analysis showed that the home is the most practical setting—yet the least supported.

CLINICAL BACKGROUND

A widespread, under-supported condition

47.2%

of neurologically disabled children experience dysphagia, while frequent guided practice remains difficult to sustain outside clinical care.

“The treatment works only when the child is willing to return to it.”

STAKEHOLDER ANALYSIS

Existing therapy scenarios & their friction

Framed through the 5W1H lens of who and where, the three stakeholders read the three common dysphagia-therapy scenarios very differently.

Hospital

Children

Ages 6–12 · bored by repetitive drills · fear medical instruments · want timely positive feedback

+ Professional facilities; standardized procedures; safe instruments.

– Unfamiliar, tense setting; tongue depressors cause fear; monotony → poor compliance.

+ More comfortable, personalized treatment.

– No standardized protocol; repetitive, monotonous → low engagement.

Parents

Cost-sensitive · lack professional skills · assisted training can stress the child

+ High credibility; direct medical guidance.

– Inconvenient commuting; long waits.

+ Flexible scheduling; more therapist contact.

– Heavy cost → hard to sustain long-term.

+ Cost-effective; convenient for daily training.

– No expertise; hard to evaluate outcomes.

Doctors / Therapists

Highly specialized · busy schedules limit long-term one-on-one guidance

+ Accurate assessment; VFSS / FEES available.

– Limited consultation time.

+ Individualized rehab with close observation.

– No hospital resources; unsystematic data.

+ Fits high-intensity, repetitive training.

– Cannot observe movements; hard to gauge effect.

Core Pain Points

LOW COMPLIANCE

Children show poor compliance across hospital, clinic and home — a clear need for engaging, self-motivated training.

INSUFFICIENT FREQUENCY

High-frequency repetitive training is hard to reach in clinical settings, so the home becomes the primary rehab setting.

HIGH PROFESSIONAL DEMAND

Methods are complex and parents lack guidance, limiting home-based effectiveness.

02 — Game

Unity Game Development

The companion app turns pressure and fingering data into musical notes, visible progress, and friendly rewards. Numbered notation lowers the learning barrier, while an owl guide provides immediate feedback without making the child feel evaluated.
